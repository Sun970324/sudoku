Swordfish

A Swordfish is when a digit's candidates in three rows all lie within the same three columns. That digit can then be removed from every other cell in those three columns.

Digits to watch
One
Lines needed
Three rows and three columns
What to remove
That digit in the rest of the three columns

The Swordfish pattern

The diagram shows digit 7 only. In rows 2, 5 and 8, every place for 7 is inside columns 1, 5 and 9. A row can have two or three of them. It is an X-Wing stretched to three lines.

Swordfish cells Cells where 7 is removed 7s that are not affected

Why does this work?

Rows 2, 5 and 8 each need a 7, and all three can only place it in columns 1, 5 and 9. A column holds only one 7, so the three rows use three different columns, and each of the three columns gets its 7 from these rows.

So the rest of columns 1, 5 and 9 (R4C1, R6C5 and R1C9) cannot be 7. Remove those candidates.

How to find the Swordfish

  1. Pick one digit.
  2. Find rows where that digit has two or three candidates.
  3. Check whether three such rows together use only three columns.
  4. If so, remove the digit from the rest of those three columns.

Rows and columns can swap. If three columns keep their candidates within three rows, remove the digit from the rest of those rows.

Common questions

Do all three rows need three candidates?

No. Two or three per row is fine, as long as the three rows together touch only three columns.

What about four lines?

The same idea gives a jellyfish: four rows whose candidates lie in four columns.

Any tips for spotting it alongside X-Wings?

Collect the rows where the digit has two candidates. Two rows on the same columns are an X-Wing, three rows on three columns are a swordfish.
